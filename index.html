How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the one above it. A ball released at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each peg, repeating that split again and again as it falls. When the final row is cleared, it drops into one of the bottom pockets, with one more pocket than there are rows.

Because each route is built from repeated fifty-fifty decisions, most balls gather around the centre and only a few drift far out to the sides. Mathematicians describe that shape as a Galton board, and the result as a binomial distribution. In plain terms, the middle pockets are common and the outer ones are rare, so the payout table mirrors that curve in reverse.

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes get larger while their chance of landing gets smaller. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.
